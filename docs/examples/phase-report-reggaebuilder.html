ReggaeBuilder Phase Report

The goal was a mix that stays wide in stereo but holds up when summed to mono. A new meter measured each stem and the master, a linear simulator predicted what width changes would do, and an optimizer chose the smallest set of changes that meets the agreed targets. Three Utility devices made the change. Correlation and mono sum were checked against full-song renders before and after.

03

Measurement method

The meter (analysis/phase_meter.py) reads each exported stem and the main mix. It runs an STFT with an 8192-sample Hann window and a 4096-sample hop: 5.4 Hz bins, 93 ms frames at 44.1 kHz. Per frame and per band it accumulates L², R² and L·R*. Everything else follows from those three sums.

M = (L + R) / 2    S = (L − R) / 2
ρ = Re Σ L·R* / √(Σ|L|² · Σ|R|²)   phase correlation, −1 … +1
mono loss = 10·log₁₀( ΣM² / (ΣM² + ΣS²) ) dB   level change when folded to mono
width = ΣS² / (ΣM² + ΣS²)   0 = mono, 50% = uncorrelated, 100% = anti-phase
balanced L/R ⇒ mono loss = 10·log₁₀((1 + ρ)/2): ρ 0.71 → −0.69 dB, ρ 0.84 → −0.36 dB

Fully uncorrelated stereo (ρ = 0) folds down 3 dB. That is not cancellation; the two channels just stop adding coherently. Real cancellation, where content disappears in mono, shows up as ρ < 0 and a loss beyond −3 dB. For that reason the meter also reports short-term correlation (per frame), the 5th percentile and the share of time below zero, because a song-average figure can hide brief anti-phase moments.

In the low end the meter adds a generalized cross-correlation lag between L and R. A time offset between channels, such as a Haas delay, shows up as a lag; phase rotation from a filter does not. The pair check takes every pair of stems that carry low-frequency energy, kick and bass included. It compares their summed power with the sum of their individual powers and finds the best lag within ±10 ms. That is how you'd catch a bass that thins the kick.

04

Diagnosis: what was wrong and what wasn't

Wrong

  • Lead: ρ = 0.05, below zero 41% of the time, and −3.6 dB mono loss in 150–300 Hz. It was a wide synth with its fundamental in the low mids.
  • B-Delay return: ρ = −0.02, below zero 50% of the time. A ping-pong-style stereo delay is close to uncorrelated by design, and in bars 113–120 it carried 40% of the main's side energy at 300 Hz–1 kHz.
  • Acid Bass: only ρ = 0.61, but it is loud. In 300 Hz–1 kHz it owned 38–59% of the side energy in bars 121–144 and nearly all of it in bars 97–112.
  • Main 300 Hz–1 kHz: ρ = 0.51 over a band holding 45% of the energy, and 0.19 in the worst 8-bar section.

Not wrong

  • Low end: ρ ≥ 0.999 below 150 Hz, L/R lag 0.00 ms. Kick 1.00, Toms 0.98, 22-Analog Lab V 1.00.
  • Stem pairs: every low-end pair summed within ±0.1 dB of its power sum, with no kick/bass or tom/bass cancellation. Time-sliding tracks would have fixed nothing.
  • Filters: every Auto Filter and EQ processes L and R identically at 100% Dry/Wet. That rotates phase equally in both channels, which changes tone but not stereo correlation.
  • LFO Phase: Auto Filter's LFO Phase (Stereo mode = Phase) offsets the left and right LFOs; Offset only moves the start of the cycle. You were right about that. Only Shimmer Melody's filter had a stereo offset (183°), and Shimmer was not a main contributor.

Untouched by choice: Shaker (ρ 0.01, 5.7 dB L/R imbalance), Moody Melody (ρ 0.09) and the A-Bright Long Verb return (ρ 0.26) are wide, but quiet or decorrelated by design. The optimizer could narrow any of them and chose not to, because the main-bus targets were met without it.

05

A simulator instead of trial and error

An export takes three minutes and needs hands off Live, so a blind search through Live was out. The changes on the table are all linear and time-invariant on the M/S pair: Utility width scales S, Bass Mono is a crossover on S, polarity and Left Inv are sign flips and an M↔S swap, and a low cut scales both. So the effect of any setting on the main mix can be computed exactly from the stems that are already rendered.

X = [M₁, S₁, M₂, S₂, … , M_rest, S_rest]   per STFT bin
G[b, t, k, l] = Re Σ_bins∈b Σ_frames∈t X_k · X_l*   Gram tensor: 49 sub-bands × 160 bars × 24 × 24
M_main = Σ_k u_M[b,k] · X_k    S_main = Σ_k u_S[b,k] · X_k   u = per-band weights from the settings
E_MM = u_Mᵀ G u_M   E_SS = u_Sᵀ G u_S   E_MS = u_Mᵀ G u_S   → ρ, mono loss, width, per band and per bar
Bass Mono at f_c: side gain = 1 / (1 + (f_c/f)⁴)   Linkwitz-Riley, power-complementary

Building G takes about 25 seconds, one pass over the audio. After that, scoring any combination of settings is a handful of einsum contractions and takes milliseconds. The tensor keeps cross terms between stems, so it captures stems cancelling or reinforcing each other as well as each stem's own width.

The residual trick. The first version summed the adjustable stems with the untouched ones and came out too optimistic (300 Hz–1 kHz: simulated 0.56, real 0.51). It was missing the group buses: the Synths group has its own compression and saturation, which the raw member stems don't carry. Version 2 models the untouched part of the mix as top-level stems (group buses included) minus the adjustable stems, so the bus processing ends up in the residual. That brought the baseline within 0.02 of the real main.

06

Optimizer

The search space was agreed up front. It covered Utility width 50–120% and Bass Mono 100–300 Hz on eleven wide stems and both returns, plus Left Inv on Lead and Moody Melody, a keep-or-remove for Shimmer's existing polarity flip, Main's Bass Mono at 120–180 Hz, the Acid Bass chorus high-pass and a reverb low cut. The targets became soft constraints, each a squared hinge:

J(p) = Σ_targets w_i · max(0, t_i − m_i(p))² / σ_i²   +   R(p)
m_i: <150 Hz ρ ≥ 0.98, 150–300 Hz ρ ≥ 0.85, 300 Hz–1 kHz ρ ≥ 0.6, mono loss ≥ −0.8 dB,
     per-bar ρ ≥ 0.05, 1–10 kHz side drop ≤ 2 dB, per-stem band loss ≥ −3 dB where band > 2% of stem energy
R(p) = Σ √((1 − w)² + ε) + 0.5·(f_c − 60)/240 + 0.3·[switch on]   smallest change wins

The continuous parameters were solved with L-BFGS-B (bounded quasi-Newton, numerical gradients, step 10⁻³, at most 60 iterations). The on/off switches can't be differentiated, so all 2³ = 8 combinations were enumerated, each with its own continuous solve. Left Inv was offered only on stems whose side energy is at least as large as their mid, since that's the only case where swapping M and S helps. A snap step then rounds widths to 1%, sets widths within 3% of 100% back to 100%, and turns off any crossover below 100 Hz.

Switch combinations, round 1 (whole-song targets)
Switches onobjective Jiterations

Every switch costs 0.3 in regularization, so subtracting 0.3 per switch shows what each one does to the metrics. Shimmer polarity: exactly +0.300, so flipping it changes nothing. Shimmer is uncorrelated with the rest of the mix, and inverting both channels of an uncorrelated stem leaves the sum's power unchanged. Lead Left Inv: +0.169, so the metrics improved by 0.13 but not enough to pay for the switch. Moody Left Inv: +0.429, so the metrics got 0.13 worse. Their side and mid are close in level, so swapping M and S moves energy around rather than narrowing anything. Round 1 result: Acid Bass 81%, Lead 89%, B-Delay 94%. B-Delay was dropped because the CLI can't reach returns, and with the returns held fixed the optimizer gave the same 81/89 split.

07

Round 1 check: fragment A/B

To save time and disk, round 1 was checked on a 34-bar fragment (bars 111–144) instead of a full export. The first comparison against the full R0 render was confounded: Lead came out about 2.5 dB quieter in the fragment. Analog Lab's free-running LFOs and arps restart at the render start, so a fragment is not the same audio as the same bars in a full render. The fix was a clean A/B: the same fragment rendered twice, once with the new Utilities bypassed (R1A) and once with them on (R1).

08

Sections: the song average hid the bad part

A song-wide target can be met while one section is badly out. The optimizer gained a section mode: the 150–300 Hz and 300 Hz–1 kHz targets apply to every 8-bar section, each penalty weighted by that section's share of the band's energy. Run against R0, it found bars 113–144 as the problem. The side energy there came from different sources in different parts.

Who owns the side signal, 300 Hz – 1 kHz

Each stem's share of the side-signal energy in each 8-bar section, computed from the Gram tensor's diagonal (cross terms left out). Bars 113–120 were a B-Delay problem; bars 121–144 an Acid Bass problem. The Lead was a steady 15–22%. After the change, the "rest of mix" share grows because the three narrowed stems shrink; the absolute side level in this band fell 3.1 dB.

Incidents

  • Live crashed during the first final export (crash report at 11:46, a minute after the export started). Crash recovery restored the Acid Bass and Lead Utilities at the right values but reset the B-Delay Utility to 100%. It was set to 60% again before re-exporting.
  • Drift check after the crash. Every track's mixer, device list, on/off state and native device parameters were compared between the saved .als (read only) and the live set. Each difference traced to automation read at the playhead, which sat at bar 121 instead of bar 1 (B-Delay Feedback/Dry-Wet, Aliens and Hat Rolls Delay on/off, Lead/Shimmer/crash volumes, Shimmer pan) or to the API reporting normalized values where the file stores real units. Nothing else had drifted.
  • Export path. The second export wrote stems loose in the project folder instead of a subfolder, and named the return "B-Delay | Utility". The files were moved to PhaseFinal\ and the return renamed to match R0.
  • Fragment confound in round 1, covered in 07.
